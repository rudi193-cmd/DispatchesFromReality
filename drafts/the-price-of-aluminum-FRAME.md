## The fact the whole thing turns on

The metal never changed. The aluminum on Napoleon III's table (if the story is even true) and the aluminum in a recycling bin are the same atoms. What changed between 1854 and 1900 was the **process**, and the price fell more than thirtyfold in the sixteen years after the Washington Monument tip alone.

Scarcity was never in the ground. It was in the method.

## Candidate theses

### A. Scarcity is made, not found
Value sat with whoever controlled the process: Deville's sodium, then Hall's patent, then Pittsburgh Reduction, then the power and the cryolite. This continues the [Nobody Adds It Up](../essays/nobody-adds-it-up/README.md) pattern: control sits one layer up from the thing itself. Here the layer is a process instead of an account.
- **Strength:** fits the existing body of work; the evidence is solid at the `fitted` level.
- **Risk:** aluminum is a story of scarcity *ending*, which runs the opposite way from the chokepoint essays. That could be a feature: the chokepoint moved instead of disappearing (to electricity, to Ivittuut, to Alcoa's patent era).

### B. The price of intelligence
Cognition is getting cheap the way aluminum did. What happens to the people and institutions whose status rested on it being scarce? And where are the new "hydropower" and "cryolite", meaning the energy and the single-source inputs everything quietly depends on?
- **Strength:** timely, and the energy parallel (aluminum as stored electricity, inference as stored electricity) is direct.
- **Risk:** the most crowded take there is. It needs a specific claim the history *earns*, not a mood.

### C. The wonder we lose
The capstone of the national monument and a soda can are the same material. What becomes of awe once something gets cheap? This is quieter and more personal, and closer to the Facebook-video origin than to an argument.
- **Strength:** honest to how the piece actually started.
- **Risk:** soft unless it lands somewhere concrete.

### D. The myth outlives the metal
The lore (Napoleon's cutlery, the crown jewels, Roman aluminum) is repeated far more than the documented history. People keep the parable and lose the process. The Tiberius story, a myth about aluminum that is not about aluminum, is the strongest case.
- **Strength:** uses the checking work itself as the material, which is very much this repo's method.
- **Risk:** could read as debunking for its own sake.

### E. Some metal: the words in the web
This one ties to *Some Pig*, the boxes-and-escape paper (`research/boxes-and-escape/`, still on the drafts PR). Wilbur never changes. "He never leaves the pen. He leaves the category," and four words of well-placed copy do it, delivered in a container that makes them credible. Aluminum is the same move, run by the press and by showmen instead of a spider. The metal never changed either. What changed it, before the process did, was where it was shown and what was said about it:
- **The container made the claim.** The monument tip went into Tiffany's window before it went onto the monument (without permission, per the National Park Service). Bars went into the 1855 Paris Exposition, and the retellings moved them "next to the crown jewels." A shop window and an exhibition hall do what Charlotte's web does: they make the message about the subject's worth. Charlotte also does her work at a livestock exhibition.
- **The copy outlived the writing.** *Some Pig* can recall SOME PIG, TERRIFIC, RADIANT, HUMBLE and not one sentence of White's prose. Aluminum's equivalent is the Napoleon III cutlery: the least-sourced story is the one everybody can recite, and the documented history (Deville's sodium, Hall's postmarked letters) is the part nobody remembers. That is thesis D seen from the media side.
- **The campaign is still running.** The piece began with a Facebook video retelling the same lore 170 years on. The repetition is the evidence.
- **Checked:** see the bridge section below. Verne's aluminum projectile (1865) and the 1856 *Household Words* "loam-silver" item are confirmed. "Silver from clay" and "age of aluminum" are not, and are dropped.
- **Strength:** links this piece to existing work. Hype then becomes a mechanism (container plus repetition) rather than a complaint about the media.
- **Risk:** *Some Pig* is unpublished. If this piece leans on it, the order of publication matters.

These can combine. **A + D + E** is the closest fit with the repo: who controlled the process, the copy that made the metal precious before the process made it cheap, and how the copy outlived the history.

### The bridge image: the corporate Charlotte's web (Sean's)

A tech-company spider above the AI, spinning in silicon and aluminum thread: SOME AI. CAREFUL, MIGHT BITE. OH GOD IT'S GOING TO KILL US BEFORE YEAR'S END.

What the image carries:
- **The thread is literal.** Chips are silicon and data centers are full of aluminum, and both run on the same cheap power. *Verified (`fitted`, from excerpts):* the Pacific Northwest once had about 10 hydro-fed smelters (about 40% of **US** output in the 1980s). The last, Intalco in Ferndale, WA, was idled in 2020 and was being demolished in March 2026. Google opened its data center at The Dalles in 2006 and in 2016 bought land on a former smelter site there. Smelters still take roughly three-quarters of Iceland's electricity (2012–13 figures), and in 2021 Landsvirkjun curtailed both smelters and data centers and refused new crypto loads. Quebec now requires ministerial approval for any new load of 5 MW or more, and in 2026 proposed a data-center tariff about double the industrial rate. Globally, smelting used about 900 TWh against data centers' about 415 TWh (2024, IEA), so smelting is still roughly twice as large. **Hedge:** this is "same regions, same cheap power", not head-to-head bidding. Alcoa's CFO, Molly Beerman, told the Citi 2025 Basic Materials Conference (3 Dec 2025) that US smelters "typically pay $30 to $40 per megawatt hour" while data centers "are prepared to pay more than $100" (trade-press report read 2026-10-12, `fitted`). Note the wording is *typically pay*, not *need*. The earlier gloss that Alcoa is "not directly competing" for its existing plants is **not in the passage read**. Until it is found, section IX rests on the price gap alone and does not quote it.
- **Praise and doom are the same copy.** Charlotte's words don't need to be flattering, only to make the subject *significant*. "It might kill us" raises the pig's category as surely as "some pig" does. A thing that could end the world is not an ordinary pig, and whatever it is, it's worth a great deal. *Verified (`fitted`, from excerpts):* Charlotte's second word, **TERRIFIC**, entered English in Milton's *Paradise Lost* (1667, VII.497, of the serpent) meaning *causing terror*. It weakened to "very great, excessive" by 1809 and became "excellent" by the late 19th century (OED has 1871, Etymonline 1888, so don't give a single year). Merriam-Webster still lists the "fit to excite fear or awe" sense, so both meanings were in the dictionaries in 1952. **No critic was found commenting on White's double meaning.** That makes the observation ours to make, but we can't cite anyone for it. In the book the goose suggests the word and Wilbur protests "I'm not terrific"; it goes into the web in chapter 13. The book uses it purely as praise.
- **The spider has moved.** Charlotte works because nobody thinks "some spider". The web hides its author, so the message is taken to be about the pig. In the corporate version the spider writes about its own pig, and the web still works, because the reader's attention lands on the pig, not on who is spinning.
- **Aluminum shows how this ends.** The hype came *before* the process. *Verified:* Verne's Gun Club makes its moon projectile of aluminum (*From the Earth to the Moon*, 1865, ch. VII), with Barbicane noting the price "has fallen now to nine dollars a pound" (now `measured`), and arguing that it is "very widely distributed, forming the base of most of the rocks", which is the abundance paradox stated in 1865. In 1856 Dickens's *Household Words* joked about "loam-silver" and predicted the metal would reach household use. "The Metal of the Future" headlines appear in 1890 and 1892. The Eros statue at Piccadilly Circus (1893) is cast aluminum. **Drop or hedge:** "silver from clay" as an 1855 label (no primary source found); "metal of the future" in Verne (not found); "age of aluminum" as 19th-century hype (not found). Then the woodshed made the metal common, and the words stopped mattering. If cognition is going the way aluminum went, the words in the web are the precious-phase copy, and the open question is what they're worth after the collapse.
- **Do not overclaim.** The piece doesn't need to argue that anyone's warnings are insincere. Charlotte believed every word she wrote. The mechanism works the same whether the spider means it or not, and that's the more interesting claim, and the one that can be defended.

This image could be the hinge in option 2 or 3: aluminum supplies the history, *Some Pig* supplies the mechanism, and the corporate web is where both arrive.

**Three ways to put them together:**
1. **Fold aluminum into *Some Pig*** as a third case. Risk: *Some Pig* is already about 4,800 words across eight sections, and aluminum's process half would have nowhere to sit.
2. **Companion pieces**, each standing alone and citing the other. *Some Pig* = reception and format, *Price of Aluminum* = value, copy and process. This keeps the scope of each piece clean.
3. **Aluminum takes the spine. ← CHOSEN.** It becomes the lead piece because its evidence is easier to check than *Some Pig*'s (which is marked unverified throughout), and *Some Pig* becomes the deeper follow-up on machines.
