## 1. OED — *terrific* · Section IX

- **Where:** oed.com, entry *terrific, adj.* It needs a subscription; most public libraries give access with a library card.
- **Claim as it stands:** the word entered English in Milton's *Paradise Lost* (1667, VII.497, of the serpent: "with brazen Eyes / And hairie Main terrific") meaning *causing terror*. It weakened to "very great, excessive" by 1809 and meant "excellent" by the late 19th century. Sources disagree on the date: an 1871 *Athenaeum* citation in the OED, and 1888 per Etymonline.
- **Confirm:**
  - [ ] Sense 1 wording, and that the first citation is *PL* VII.497, 1667
  - [ ] The date and source of the first "excellent / very good" citation
  - [ ] Whether the OED marks the fear sense obsolete or rare, and its last citation date (was it still live around 1952?)
- **Optional:** does any criticism of *Charlotte's Web* remark on the double sense? None was found. If none exists, the observation is ours, so state it without a citation.

## 2. National Park Service — "The Point of the Matter" · Section V

- **Where:** https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/wamocap.htm. As a cross-check, also read "Topping Off the Tip", *Prologue* (National Archives, 2014).
- **Claim as it stands:** the cap was set 6 Dec 1884: 100 oz (about 2.835 kg), 8.9 in tall. Cast by William Frishmuth of Philadelphia, it was shown at Tiffany's in New York *without permission* before installation and called the largest aluminum casting of its time. It was part of the lightning protection (the original spec was platinum-plated copper or bronze). Aluminum then cost about $1.10/oz, roughly the price of silver. Frishmuth charged $225 per NPS, though *Prologue* says the price rose late from $100. Lightning cracked the stone below the cap in June 1885.
- **Confirm:**
  - [ ] Each figure above as NPS states it
  - [ ] The "without permission" wording: NPS's own, or inferred?
  - [ ] The cost as NPS gives it, and the *Prologue* account of the price change

## 3. Verne — *From the Earth to the Moon*, chapter VII · Section IV

- **Where:** Project Gutenberg eBook #83 (https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/83), chapter VII, "The Hymn of the Cannon-Ball". Note which translation the Gutenberg text uses, because wording varies between translations.
- **Claim as it stands:** the Gun Club chooses aluminum for the projectile. The Major objects to the cost, and Barbicane replies that the price "has fallen now to nine dollars a pound". A 108-inch shell with 12-inch walls would weigh 19,250 lb in aluminum against 67,440 lb in cast iron, at a cost of $173,050. The text calls aluminum "three times lighter than iron" and praises it as "white as silver, as incorrodible as gold, as tenacious as iron" (that quotation was seen only from an 1890 edition).
- **Status: READ 2026-10-12 (Sean, at first hand). Tag → `measured`.**
  - [x] Exact sentences copied. Every figure matched: the nine dollars a pound, 108 in / 12 in, 67,440 lb vs 19,250 lb, $173,050, "three times lighter than iron".
  - [x] The "white as silver, as incorrodible as gold" line is **not** in this translation, which reads "possesses the whiteness of silver, the indestructibility of gold, the tenacity of iron, the fusibility of copper, the lightness of glass". The draft now quotes this version.
  - [x] "Metal of the future" does not appear in the passage read.
  - [ ] **Still open:** record which translation was read (Gutenberg #83 credits it on the title page) so the quotation can be attributed.
  - **New find:** Barbicane states the paradox in 1865: the metal is "very widely distributed, forming the base of most of the rocks", yet costs nine dollars a pound. Use it in section II or IV.

## 5. USGS historical aluminum prices · Section VI

- **Where:** USGS Data Series 140, aluminum (historical statistics); USGS SIR 2012-5188 (metal prices in the US through 2010). For pre-1900 prices, the *Mineral Resources of the United States* annual volumes, 1883–1900 (on pubs.usgs.gov and HathiTrust).
- **Claim as it stands** (nominal US$; everything except 1884 is `assumed`):

  | Year | Current figure |
  |------|----------------|
  | 1852 | about $545 / lb |
  | 1884 | about $1.10 / oz (about $16–18 / lb) |
  | 1888 | $4.86 / lb |
  | 1893 | 78¢ / lb |
  | 1897 | 36¢ / lb |
  | 1900 | under 50¢ / lb |

  The derived claim is that the price fell **more than thirtyfold, 1884–1900**.
- **Confirm:**
  - [ ] Each year's figure as USGS gives it (USGS will likely have nothing for 1852; if so, leave it `assumed`)
  - [ ] The units and the basis (per lb or per kg; producer or market price)
  - [ ] Whether the thirtyfold claim survives the corrected endpoints
