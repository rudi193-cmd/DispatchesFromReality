# The Fever Chart in Bursts

**Sean Campbell** · Working draft · November 2026

**Tags:** `crane-brinton`, `anatomy-of-revolution`, `thermidor`, `dual-sovereignty`, `j-curve`, `networked-movements`, `self-organized-criticality`, `provenance`, `systems-thinking`

---

I have owned *The Anatomy of Revolution* since the late 1990s. The copy is soft at the corners and broken at the spine in the places I keep going back to, which are the chapter on the moderates and the chapter on Thermidor. I have been reading the present against it for a long time, and I finished this draft on election night, November 3, 2026, with results still coming in.

This paper does three things. It states plainly what Brinton claimed, because his framework gets quoted far more often than it gets read. It sets out a reading of the United States from roughly 2008 to now that I have been building for years, and it says where that reading is strong and where it is weak. And it argues that the fever chart needs one structural revision to describe a revolution in a networked society: the process no longer arrives as one continuous fever. It arrives in bursts that spread fast and fade, while something underneath keeps accumulating.

This is analysis, not a forecast. Brinton himself warned against reading his chart as a forecasting tool, and I hold the same line.

---

## What Brinton actually claimed

Brinton published the book in 1938 and revised it in 1952 and again, expanded, in 1965. He compared four revolutions he considered successful: the English revolution of the 1640s, the American, the French, and the Russian. The 1938 first edition is now in the public domain in the United States and is on Project Gutenberg (eBook 78065), which is the text I quote below. `measured`: I read the passages quoted here in that text.

His organizing device is borrowed from medicine. He calls it a convenience, offered "with no implications of eternal and absolute validity." In the generation before the outbreak there are prodromal signs, signals that a disease is on its way but is not yet the disease. Then the fever begins, works up "not regularly but with advances and retreats" to a crisis marked by the rule of the most violent revolutionists, and then comes convalescence, "usually marked by a relapse or two." The patient survives, possibly strengthened, but "certainly not wholly made over into a new man."

The stages inside that chart are the ones people remember: the old regime, a brief honeymoon, the rule of the moderates, the accession of the extremists, the Reign of Terror and Virtue, and Thermidor.

Three parts of the book matter more for this paper than the stage sequence does.

**The signs are common.** At the end of his chapter on the old regimes, Brinton lists the preliminary signs he found: government deficits, complaints over taxation, government favoring one set of economic interests over another, administrative confusion, the desertion of the intellectuals, loss of self-confidence in the ruling class, members of that class coming to believe their own privileges are unjust, intensified social antagonism, blocked careers in the professions and white-collar work, and a separation of economic power from political power and social standing. Then he concedes the hard part himself: some, if not most, of these signs "may be found in almost any modern society at any time." What mattered in four, or at least three, of his cases was an unusual combination and intensity, and he says diagnosis in the early stages "cannot be reduced to a neat formula." Anyone who uses this book to say a revolution is coming has skipped that page.

**Dual sovereignty is the real test.** Brinton says the uniformity that runs deeper than the left-ward sequence of power is what theorists and historians of the Russian revolution called *dvoevlastie*, dual power, which he prefers to render as dual sovereignty. A normal society has one coordinated chain of institutions through which conflicts are finally settled in action. Dual sovereignty exists when "another and conflicting chain of institutions provides another and conflicting set of decisions," so that two sets of institutions, leaders, and laws demand obedience across ordinary life. He is careful about scale. Mass nullification of Prohibition, he says, was not dual sovereignty. A labor federation enforcing its own laws in factories and taking over policing and markets would be. And he notes that the people building the rival chain often do not think they are supplanting the legal government. They think of themselves as supplementing it, or even preserving it.

**The moderates fail as realists, not idealists.** The usual story is that the moderates were good people crushed by a hard world. Brinton calls that "peculiarly misleading." He argues they failed because they used grand words the way normal politicians use them, without intending to pursue them to their conclusions, at exactly the moment when only people with at least a touch of fanatic idealism, or the ability to act the part, could attain leadership.

The standard critiques belong here too, before I build anything on him. The four cases are all revolutions that succeeded, so the pattern is drawn from the outcome it describes. Theda Skocpol's *States and Social Revolutions* (1979) moved the field toward structural explanations, state breakdown under international pressure and agrarian class structure, and away from the natural-history approach Brinton represents. The book is a comparative description, not a causal model, and it fits its own four cases better than later ones.

## The reading: a fever that started in 2008

This is the thesis I have been carrying. It is mine, not Brinton's, and it is contested.

**Old regime cracking, 2008 to 2016.** The financial crisis of 2008 is the closest thing to Brinton's fiscal and legitimacy signs. The federal deficit for fiscal 2009 reached $1.4 trillion, 9.9 percent of GDP, up from 3.1 percent the year before (Congressional Budget Office), and Washington committed up to $700 billion to financial institutions through TARP. Meanwhile median family net worth fell 38.8 percent between 2007 and 2010 (Federal Reserve, Survey of Consumer Finances), and a record 2.9 million properties received foreclosure filings in 2010 (RealtyTrac). `measured`, except the foreclosure count, which is `fitted` from a press report of RealtyTrac's figures. One correction to how I used to tell this: by the Budget Office's final accounting, TARP's bank programs were repaid at a net gain of about $9 billion, and its net cost, about $31 billion, came from the housing programs. The grievance was about who was rescued first and on what terms, not about the final bill. That is still Brinton's sign of a government favoring one set of economic interests, but it rests on timing and terms, not on cost.

Over the following years other signs on his list showed up beside it. Public confidence fell and stayed down: Gallup's confidence in banks went from 41 percent in 2007 to 22 percent in 2009 and was still 27 percent in 2016, and confidence in Congress was 9 percent in 2016. Pew's long series on trust in the federal government ran between 10 and 26 percent from 2009 to 2011, though that series had been falling for decades before 2008, so it shows a low floor more than a break. `measured`. Brinton's sign is the ruling class losing its own self-confidence, which no survey measures; what these numbers show is the public withdrawing its confidence, which is related but not the same thing.

Blocked careers and debt fell hardest on the young. Student loan balances roughly doubled, from $0.64 trillion at the end of 2008 to $1.31 trillion at the end of 2016 (Federal Reserve Bank of New York, Quarterly Report on Household Debt and Credit). Unemployment among recent college graduates more than doubled, from 3.4 percent at the end of 2007 to 7.9 percent at the end of 2010, and the share working in jobs that did not require a degree peaked at 47.4 percent in early 2012 (Federal Reserve Bank of New York, The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates). `measured`. That underemployment figure is less dramatic than it sounds: the series has sat in the 40s for decades and hit 48.4 percent in 1992.

The desertion of the intellectuals cannot be counted, so I will name examples instead of claiming a trend. Economists David Autor, David Dorn and Gordon Hanson showed that the China trade shock left local job losses that lingered for at least a decade, a challenge from inside the field to its settled view of how labor markets adjust (National Bureau of Economic Research, 2016; `measured`, from the abstract). On the left, Christopher Hayes's *Twilight of the Elites* (2012) argued that meritocracy had failed on its own terms, a member of the credentialed class indicting it; on the right, Charles Murray's *Coming Apart* (2012) described an upper class pulling away from everyone else. `fitted`, from summaries of both books.

In 2016 the Republican establishment lost its nomination to Donald Trump, who took 45.5 percent of the Republican primary vote, and on the Democratic side Bernie Sanders took 42.8 percent of the primary vote against the party's favorite (Federal Election Commission, primary results; `measured`, caucuses not included, which understates Sanders). One establishment was defeated and the other was seriously challenged, which is a weaker claim than I used to make and the one the numbers support.

One of Brinton's signs needs a modern reading, and *Nobody Adds It Up* is where I worked it out. The last item on his list is the separation of economic power from political power and social standing. In his old regimes it was a class that held wealth but was shut out of rule, and he puts it bluntly: political power and social distinction are "the handmaids of economic power," and where they come apart, "you have a fairly reliable preliminary sign of revolution." `measured`. That essay traced a related split in a different form: the scripture kept in a language almost nobody read, the company scrip good only at the company store, the leased Bell telephone, and now the account, the library and the workspace a person believes they own while the control sits one layer up, with whoever administers the access. The power that governs much of ordinary life now sits in that layer, outside the chain of institutions anyone votes on, and the people living under it hold their things on terms someone else can change. I am not claiming that is the same separation Brinton described. It is the same shape at a different joint, and it belongs on the list of signs I have been watching combine.

**The rule of the moderates, 2021 to 2025.** I read the Biden administration as Brinton's moderate phase. It came to power promising restoration and normalcy, governed through the inherited institutions, and was squeezed from both sides, by a radical faction that had just held power and by an energized left that read every compromise as proof the system could be pushed further. Brinton's description fits uncomfortably well: the legal government is unpopular "just because it is an obvious and responsible government," because it has to shoulder some of the old regime's unpopularity.

**The accession, 2025 onward.** The second Trump administration, on this reading, is the faction that took power after the moderates failed. The part of Brinton's sequence that matters most is not the policy agenda. It is whether power begins to move outside the coordinated chain of institutions.

The strongest evidence for that is in the record of court compliance. An Associated Press review of court records, published at the start of May 2026, found that in the second administration's first 15 months, district judges ruled the government was violating an order in at least 31 lawsuits, about one in every eight cases in which courts had at least temporarily blocked an administration action. That count is in addition to more than 250 instances of noncompliance that judges flagged in individual immigration petitions. Legal scholars and former federal judges told the AP they could recall at most a few violations across the full terms of other recent administrations, including Trump's first. `fitted`: the AP review as carried by syndicated copies; I did not read the court records.

The strongest evidence against it is in the same review. In 15 of those 31 lawsuits, an appellate court or the Supreme Court either allowed the underlying policy or limited the lower court's attempt to correct the violation, and the administration eventually backed down in about a third of the cases. The White House position is that higher courts overturned unlawful district rulings and that it complies with lawful ones. `fitted`, same source.

Read against Brinton's dual sovereignty test, this is where my thesis needs the most care. What the AP describes is not a rival chain of institutions outside the state. It is a contest inside the legal chain itself, an executive treating one tier of the judiciary as non-binding while the top tier frequently sides with it. That is an inversion of Brinton's pattern. In his cases the extremists ran the illegal government and the moderates held the legal one. In mine, the faction in power holds the legal government and is testing how far its decisions can stand against parts of that same government. I think that inversion is the most interesting finding in this draft, and I do not think Brinton's categories settle it. People who reject my reading would say it is aggressive constitutional politics, contested in court and mostly resolved there, which is what checks and balances are for.

## Thermidor comes from inside

The passage I return to most is this one, from the Thermidor chapter of the 1938 text. `measured`.

> The fall of Robespierre in France had been brought about largely by a conspiracy among outwardly orthodox Jacobin deputies to the Convention, men for the most part seriously involved in war-profiteering, parliamentary corruption, stock speculation and other activities unworthy of citizens of the Republic of Virtue. Fear of the "Incorruptible" Robespierre seems to have been one of the main reasons for their action. They were successful, not unaided by Robespierre's lack of political wisdom.

Brinton adds that the Thermidoreans had apparently not meant to end the Terror at all. Executing Robespierre was, to them, one more guillotining. It became the end of the Terror because public opinion "got to work" and made it clear people were finished with it.

The mechanism is the useful part. The radical phase did not end because the opposition won. It ended when people inside the radical coalition decided the leader had become a greater danger to their own survival than breaking with him was, and when an exhausted public refused to let the reaction stop.

In 2026 that mechanism has a modern, much milder version. Through the year, Republican members of the House and Senate broke ranks with the administration over the Iran war, a proposed anti-weaponization fund, and Ukraine aid, and raised objections to nominations. In May, Senate Republicans left for recess without voting on a roughly $70 billion immigration enforcement package amid a revolt over that fund. They passed it in early June, after the Justice Department said the fund would not go forward, with no language killing it. That sequence is itself a small model of the problem: the break was real, and it bent rather than stopped the agenda. A Politico poll in October found that one in four Trump voters thought Republican candidates should break with the president to win in November. `fitted`, from press reports of each event.

The analogy has limits I want stated, not hidden. The Thermidoreans acted because the alternative was the guillotine. Today's defectors are worried about losing seats, which is a weaker motive and easier to reverse. Not everyone reads the breaks as durable: Jonathan Entin, a professor emeritus of law at Case Western, cautioned in June against overstating the Iran war powers vote as a lasting split, and an analysis of voting records published in February, before the spring and summer breaks, argued that the group of Republicans who repeatedly break with the president could shrink to about six, possibly three, after this cycle. And the casting is contested. Robespierre's power came from being incorruptible. The president's supporters cast him as the outsider purging a corrupt establishment, which makes him the Robespierre figure; his critics point to ethics conflicts and reverse the roles. Which Thermidor story a reader sees depends on who they think the corrupt party is.

There is a second reason the casting matters less than it looks, and it comes from my own earlier work. In *Post-WWII Institutional Legacies* I argued that continuity does not require sameness: the people, the agencies and the moral language all changed after Paperclip, while the land, the facilities, the procurement relationships and the contractor ecosystems carried straight through. Brinton saw the same thing from inside a revolution. His moderates find the institutions they inherit "annoyingly persistent, extremely difficult to blot out," and his convalescent patient is "certainly not wholly made over into a new man." `measured`, both lines from the 1938 text. Put those together and Thermidor looks less like a reversal than like the substrate reasserting itself once the people who were straining against it lose their nerve. That is a useful correction for my own thesis. Whatever the accession is trying to move outside the legal chain, it is pushing against the same kind of persistent substrate the Paperclip paper traced, and the most likely outcome is the one Brinton describes: changed, but not made over.

## Suffering does not start revolutions

The second passage I keep is about what happened after Thermidor. `measured`.

> When the Thermidoreans in France abandoned price-fixing and food-rationing, prices rocketed, paper money went on its classic decline, and the poor were left in a very bad way. There seems to be a general agreement that there was more actual suffering in France in the winters of 1795 and 1796 than at any other time in the revolutionary era. Yet save for a few pathetic bread riots at Paris and in some of the large cities, riots easily put down by the government, nothing happened.

He makes the same observation about the kulak liquidation and the famine of the first Five Year Plan in Russia. And he offers three explanations without choosing among them: suffering "is not in itself a spur to effective revolt"; the new ruling class uses force more effectively than the old one did; and by Thermidor most people are "worn out, exhausted, fed up."

This lines up with work before and after him. Tocqueville observed in *The Old Regime and the Revolution* (1856) that the regions that became the Revolution's chief centres were those where improvement had been most visible, not the most backward ones. James C. Davies formalized the idea in "Toward a Theory of Revolution" (*American Sociological Review*, 1962), the J-curve: revolutions are most likely when a long period of rising conditions is followed by a sharp reversal, so that expectations keep climbing while reality drops away from them. The fuel is frustrated hope. Despair, especially after upheaval, tends to sedate.

That produces the question this paper cannot answer and should not pretend to. When I describe the mood in my own circles on the left, the honest answer is both: hope that keeps getting blocked, and people getting worn down. In Brinton's terms that split is the unstable configuration. The exhausted stop defending the system but do not fight for anything else, which leaves the field to whoever still has energy.

## The revision: bursts and pooling

Here is where I think the fever chart needs changing for a networked society.

Brinton's revolutions were continuous. Each had a visible sequence and a crisis point that contemporaries could name. What I have watched since 2008 does not look like that. It looks like a series of bursts, each spreading faster and farther than any movement in his cases could, and each fading without a resolution: Occupy, the 2011 uprisings abroad, the 2020 protests, and many smaller ones.

Zeynep Tufekci's *Twitter and Tear Gas* (Yale University Press, 2017) gives the best account of why bursts fade. Networked tools let a crowd form within days, as at Gezi Park in 2013, where the 1963 March on Washington took six months of preparation, two of them with hundreds of people organizing full time (chapter 3). Those months were not overhead. They built what she calls network internalities: the trust, decision-making habits and ability to delegate that a group gains by solving problems together over time. Those become the movement's capacity, which she divides into three kinds: narrative capacity (telling its story on its own terms), disruptive capacity (interrupting the normal working of authority), and electoral or institutional capacity (threatening politicians' careers or forcing institutions to change) (chapter 8). A march is a signal of those capacities, and its power comes from what it says the crowd could do next. Digital tools let a movement produce the signal, a huge crowd, without first building the capacity it used to stand for, and once the shock wore off, governments learned to read the difference. The same speed leaves movements without ways to make collective decisions, so after the first protest they cannot change tactics, negotiate or set a next goal. She calls this a tactical freeze, and her central example is Occupy, which could not agree on a next phase after the Zuccotti Park eviction. `measured`: read in the Creative Commons edition of the book.

Erica Chenoweth's data point the same way, though not wholly independently, since her account of digital organizing cites Tufekci. In "The Future of Nonviolent Resistance" (*Journal of Democracy*, July 2020) she counts 96 new nonviolent campaigns to remove a government or win independence in 2010 to 2019, against a previous record of 60 in the decade before. Their success rate fell at the same time: above 40 percent in every decade from the 1960s, as high as 65 percent in the 1990s, but under 34 percent since 2010. She grants that states have learned "politically savvy approaches to repression," but she finds the strongest explanation in the movements themselves. They are smaller at their peaks (1.3 percent of the population since 2010, against 2.7 percent in the 1990s), they lean on street demonstrations and digital organizing instead of strikes and long-term organizing, and more than half had violent flanks in the 2010s, against 30 to 35 percent before. Her remedy is planning, training and coalition-building before mass mobilization. `measured`: read in the full essay.

Both findings support the "fizzle" half of my observation. My addition is the other half, and Tufekci herself supplies its first piece of evidence: she notes that Occupy, which failed on every capacity except the narrative one, changed how the country talked about the 99 percent, and that some of its energy came back in the 2016 Sanders campaign. `measured`, chapter 8. Each burst fades as an event, but it does not reset the system to zero. It leaves something behind: networks of people who now know each other, skills learned under pressure, grievances that were named and not resolved, and people who changed their minds and did not change them back. In systems terms, the flows spike and drop while a stock keeps accumulating.

That changes which model fits. A fever has a course. A pooling system behaves more like the sandpile in Bak, Tang, and Wiesenfeld's work on self-organized criticality (*Physical Review Letters*, 1987): grains are added one at a time, most do nothing visible, and the pile organizes itself toward a critical state in which one grain can set off a slide of almost any size. The size of the slide is set by everything that piled up before it, not by the grain that triggers it. If that is the right model, then two practical conclusions follow. There will be no clean moment that announces itself as the crisis, which is part of why it is so hard to know what to do. And the people who matter most when a slide comes are the ones who already built trust at a local level, because they are the only capacity that does not have to be assembled during the slide.

I have watched one small version of that capacity at first hand, and wrote about it in *Somebody Has to Sit Down With You*. The moderators of an online physics community rebuilt, by hand and unpaid, the gatekeeping that institutions used to supply: provoke a little first, see how a confident claim holds up against real resistance, and when that is not enough, sit down with the person one to one. Nobody organized it during a crisis. It was built slowly, case by case, before anyone needed it, which is exactly the kind of stock that pools. The same caution applies here that I gave there: it is not a solved system, and it can go wrong in both directions. But it is evidence that the slow, local, person-to-person work Tufekci says networked movements skip is still being done, just not where anyone is counting it.

I have since tested this against the Crowd Counting Consortium's US protest data across two full presidential terms, January 2017 to January 2025, in a study I pre-registered before reading a row: four predictions, each with its rule and thresholds frozen in advance, and my own odds on each written down first. The result is mixed, and I am reporting it as it came out. The rising floor held: in the quiet months after each burst, active counties rose against the months before, by more than around comparison months, for all events and for crowds of 100 or more. But the control it rests on is thin, three comparison months, all in mid-2024, because bursts turned out to be far more common than the design assumed. Recruits from bursts came back about four fifths as often as places that started on their own, between the lines for pooling and for reset. Returning organizations missed the line by a hair, 0.7992 against 0.8. And carryover across issues could not be measured at all, because the comparison it needed had no room inside the window. `measured`, from the one run; the full result, including what a second study would change, is in the study's results. So the floor is the one piece of the stock the data show, and they show it on less footing than I wanted.

Two cautions on this section. First, pooling cuts both ways. What accumulates may be exhaustion as easily as capacity, and Brinton's 1795 is a picture of a pile that had accumulated mostly exhaustion. Second, Brinton's own suggestion that the Thermidorean ruling class "can and does use force with an effectiveness the old ruling class did not command" has an obvious modern counterpart in surveillance. People who feel watched hold back from exactly the visible, slow organizing that builds capacity. Chenoweth notes that the digital tools that gather crowds quickly also make surveillance easier, and her savvier repression and that feeling of being watched may be the same fact seen from two sides. Her own data cut against the surveillance half, though: she weighs the movements' choices more heavily than the states' learning. I am stating this as a hypothesis, not a finding.

## Where this could be wrong

The same objections I raised against Brinton apply to this paper, and I would rather list them than have a reader find them.

**Selection bias, again.** I am fitting a pattern onto a period I have lived through and have been watching for evidence of exactly this pattern. That is how a natural history turns into confirmation.

**Unfalsifiability.** Peter Turchin's *End Times* (2023) makes a structural case for 2020s instability from elite overproduction, popular immiseration, and weakening state finances and legitimacy, and he is often credited with a 2010 prediction in *Nature* that the United States would hit an instability peak around 2020. His critic William Davies, writing in the *New Statesman* in June 2023, notes that the 2010 remark was a loose aside the press inflated into prophecy, and argues the model's caveats make it hard to test. That criticism lands on any framework of this kind, mine included. A theory that can absorb a wave election, a constitutional crisis, and a decade of ordinary polarization as equally confirming is not yet a theory.

**The ordinary-politics reading.** The strongest alternative to everything above is that the United States is in a long partisan realignment, not a revolution. On that reading, 2016 and 2024 were legal elections won by an insurgent faction within one party, the court fights are a hard but normal test of executive power, and tonight's results are what midterms usually do to the party holding the White House. That reading explains most of the same evidence with fewer assumptions. My answer is that it does not explain the AP compliance count or the scale of the pooling, but I hold that answer as an argument, not a result.

## A data point, not a conclusion

At 00:10 Mountain time on November 4, the Associated Press balance-of-power tally showed Democrats at 223 House seats to Republicans' 132, with 80 seats not yet called; 53 Democrats to 42 Republicans in the Senate; and 25 Democratic to 19 Republican governors. `measured`, as a snapshot: I read the tally at that moment, and the final numbers will differ. Before the election, Republicans held the House 218 to 214 (with one independent and two vacancies) and the Senate 53 to 47, counting two independents who caucus with Democrats. `fitted`, from press reports.

In Brinton's terms, a wave election is the opposite of a revolutionary break. It is the coordinated chain of institutions doing exactly what it is supposed to do, which is to let pressure out through the system instead of around it. So tonight does not confirm my thesis or refute it. It sets up the tests that will:

- **Acceptance.** Whether the results are accepted without a rival claim to their authority. That is the dual sovereignty question, asked directly.
- **The check.** Whether the administration treats the new Congress as a legitimate check, or extends the pattern in the AP compliance record from district courts to the legislature.
- **The insiders.** Whether Republicans who distanced themselves before the election close ranks after it, or whether their self-preservation math now points toward a coordinated break.
- **Delivery.** Whether the incoming majority can produce results people can feel. Brinton's moderates failed on exactly this, and it decides whether the frustrated-hope half of the left stays inside the system.

I will add the results of those tests to this draft as they come in, tagged by provenance like everything else here.

## Claim boundaries

- This is a comparative reading against a 1938 framework, not a prediction, and not a claim that a revolution is underway.
- The stage mapping (2008 to 2016 as old regime, 2021 to 2025 as the moderates, 2025 onward as the accession) is the author's thesis. It is contested, and the strongest counter-reading is given above.
- Brinton is quoted from the 1938 first edition. The 1952 and 1965 revisions differ and are not quoted here.
- The pooling model is tested once, on one dataset, with a mixed result: one prediction held on a thin control, two were inconclusive, and one could not be measured. The surveillance hypothesis is still a proposal.
- Figures for court compliance and congressional dissent come from press reports and are tagged `fitted`. The election tally is a single snapshot and will not hold.
