# The Price of Aluminum

**Sean Campbell** · Working draft · October 2026

**Tags:** `aluminum`, `history-of-technology`, `scarcity`, `process`, `hall-heroult`, `energy`, `provenance`, `myth-checking`

---

I was scrolling Facebook and landed on a ten-minute science-and-history video about aluminum. I'd have called it a stupid video if you'd asked me before it started. By the end I knew something I hadn't known before: the metal in a soda can was once a luxury good. An emperor showed it off. The tip of the Washington Monument is made of it because, when the monument was finished, aluminum was precious.

I never knew any of that. That stayed with me more than the facts did. A piece of history this odd had been sitting in plain sight, in every kitchen drawer, and it took a Facebook video for me to find it.

This draft is the history first. Before I argue anything with it I want to know which parts of the story hold up. Aluminum history is told a lot, and a lot of it is told wrong. What follows is the version that survived checking, with each claim tagged by how well it is sourced.

**On provenance.** Tags follow the `assumed` / `fitted` / `measured` ordering from *Nobody Counts the Ad Breaks*. For this draft, **nothing is tagged `measured`**: the checking pass could not open primary pages in full and worked from excerpts of reputable sources. `fitted` means two or more independent reputable sources agree. `assumed` means a single source, or lore. The [packet](README.md) has the full ledger and the reads still owed.

## The commonest metal nobody had

Aluminum is the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust, about 8% by mass. Among all elements only oxygen and silicon are more common. The United States Geological Survey's critical-minerals assessment gives the figure, and crustal-composition work by Rudnick and Gao agrees with it. `fitted`

It is also almost never found as metal. Aluminum bonds to oxygen so tightly that nature keeps it locked in clays, feldspars and bauxite. The usual line, "never found native", is slightly wrong. Native aluminium is a recognized mineral species on the International Mineralogical Association's list, reported from a handful of strongly reducing settings such as the Tolbachik volcano fumaroles in Kamchatka. Every known occurrence is microscopic. `fitted`

So the material was everywhere and the metal was nowhere, and that gap is the whole story. People used aluminum's compounds for millennia without seeing the element. *Alum*, a double sulfate, was a mordant that fixed dyes into cloth in the ancient world, and the metal's name comes from it through the Latin *alumen*. `fitted`

## Naming it before having it

Humphry Davy was sure a metal sat inside alumina. From 1807 to 1812 he tried to free it several times and never did. He named it anyway. In 1808 he proposed **alumium**, and in his *Elements of Chemical Philosophy* of 1812 he settled on **aluminum**. The other spelling, **aluminium**, did not come from Davy. A reviewer in the *Quarterly Review* coined it in 1812, preferring a "classical" ending to match sodium and potassium. Both forms have been in use since, and Merriam-Webster's usage note and the Oxford English Dictionary trace the same sequence. `fitted`

The popular version, that the British "changed Davy's spelling", gets this backwards. Davy's own last word was the one Americans use now.

## Isolation, a few grains at a time

In 1825 the Danish physicist Hans Christian Ørsted announced a small, impure amount of metal. He made it by reducing aluminum chloride with potassium amalgam and distilling off the mercury. Friedrich Wöhler could not reproduce the result. In 1827 he reduced aluminum chloride with metallic potassium and got an impure powder. In 1845 he made globules about the size of pinheads, big enough to measure, and showed how light the metal was. The Science History Institute's *Distillations* and the American Chemical Society's *ChemMatters* give this sequence. `fitted`

Potassium was itself expensive and dangerous to make. Any aluminum made this way carried that cost.

## The sodium years

In 1854 Henri Sainte-Claire Deville presented a method to the French Académie des sciences that replaced potassium with sodium, which was cheaper. Some sources describe this as the start of commercial aluminum. That overstates it: 1854 was a laboratory result. Industrial production began in 1855–56, first at the Javel works near Paris and then at Rouen and Nanterre. `fitted`

Napoleon III paid for the work, interested in what a light metal could do for the military. The military motive is well attested. The specific claim that he wanted aluminum helmets or armor for his cuirassiers is weaker. `fitted` for the motive, `assumed` for the helmets.

Bars of the metal were exhibited at the 1855 Exposition Universelle in Paris. `fitted` They are often said to have been shown "next to the crown jewels", but that detail traces only to dealer pages copying one another. `assumed`

The best-known story is about Napoleon III's tableware. In it, the most honored guests at an imperial dinner ate with aluminum and everyone else made do with gold. It is repeated everywhere, in conflicting versions (cutlery or plates; a dinner for the King of Siam, or some other occasion), and no primary source for it surfaced. Treat it as a widely repeated anecdote, not a documented event. `assumed`

What the numbers support is more modest but still striking. In 1852 aluminum is quoted at about **$545 a pound**, which by weight is more than gold was then. That figure comes from a single magazine source. `assumed` French prices were falling through the Deville era: about 300 francs a kilogram in the mid-1850s, 240 in 1859, 120 in 1867. `assumed` "Worth more than gold" was briefly true. It was not true for long.

## The copy before the process

The metal was a public sensation long before most people could buy any. In 1856 Dickens's magazine *Household Words* joked about "loam-silver", a precious metal made out of clay, and predicted that it would end up in ordinary households. `fitted` In 1865 Jules Verne's Gun Club, in *From the Earth to the Moon*, chooses aluminum for its moon projectile in chapter VII. Barbicane's case for it is pure copy: the metal "possesses the whiteness of silver, the indestructibility of gold, the tenacity of iron, the fusibility of copper, the lightness of glass", and "seems to have been created for the express purpose of furnishing us with the material for our projectile." In the same speech he states the paradox this whole history turns on. The metal "is very widely distributed, forming the base of most of the rocks", and it still costs a fortune. When the Major objects, Barbicane says the price "was so at its first discovery, but it has fallen now to nine dollars a pound." The aluminum shell, 108 inches across with twelve-inch walls, would weigh 19,250 pounds against 67,440 in cast iron, and cost $173,050. "But fear not, my friends; the money will not be wanting." `measured` (chapter VII read at first hand; the translation still to be named)

Verne's nine dollars a pound also roughly agrees with the French prices of the time (120 francs a kilogram in 1867). That is a consistency between figures, not a confirmation of either.

Two phrases often attached to this period don't survive checking. No primary source turned up for "silver from clay" as an 1855 exhibition label; it looks like a later paraphrase of the same joke *Household Words* was making. "The age of aluminum" has no 19th-century attestation that could be found. `assumed` Headlines calling it "the metal of the future" do appear, in 1890 and 1892. `fitted`

## The silver year: 1884

On 6 December 1884 a small aluminum pyramid was set on top of the Washington Monument. It weighs **100 ounces (about 2.835 kg)** and is **8.9 inches** tall, and at the time it was called the largest aluminum casting ever made. William Frishmuth cast it in Philadelphia. Before it went up it was shown at Tiffany's in New York, without permission according to the National Park Service. It was also the tip of the lightning protection system. The Army engineers' original specification called for platinum-plated copper or bronze. `fitted`

In 1884 aluminum sold for about **$1.10 an ounce**, about the same as silver. That is the National Park Service's figure. `fitted` The tip of the national monument was made of a metal comparable to silver. It was precious, though no longer priced like gold.

What Frishmuth charged for it is disputed. The National Park Service says $225. The National Archives' *Prologue* reports a price that rose late from $100 to $225, and other accounts give $75 or an invoice of $256.10. `fitted` for the dispute, with no single figure settled.

Six months later, in June 1885, lightning cracked the stone just below the tip, and more rods were added. `fitted`

## The woodshed: 1886

On 23 February 1886, in a woodshed behind his family's house in Oberlin, Ohio, 22-year-old **Charles Martin Hall** produced aluminum by electrolysis. He dissolved aluminum oxide in molten cryolite and passed a current through it. He filed for a US patent on 9 July 1886.

In France, 23-year-old **Paul Héroult** had reached the same process independently and filed a French patent on 23 April 1886. When the two US applications collided, Hall won priority using postmarked letters he had written to his brother in February.

The two men were born in the same year, 1863, and died in the same year, 1914. It sounds invented, and it isn't. Britannica, the American Chemical Society's National Historic Chemical Landmark, and the *Bulletin for the History of Chemistry* all agree on the dates. `fitted`

Hall's older sister, **Julia Brainerd Hall**, was an Oberlin chemistry graduate who helped in the woodshed lab. Her records and testimony helped establish his priority. How large her scientific role was is a genuine historians' dispute. Martha Moore Trescott, writing in the *Journal of Chemical Education* in 1977, argued for a real scientific partnership. Norman Craig's later reading of the Oberlin archive found her annotations mostly about names and finances, which makes her a supporter, witness and record-keeper rather than co-inventor. `fitted` that the dispute exists. The draft takes no side.

Two years later the second half of the modern process arrived. In 1888 Carl Josef Bayer, working in St Petersburg, patented a way to precipitate pure alumina, the feedstock Hall's cells needed. The process is now done at industrial scale from bauxite. `fitted` That same year Hall and his backers founded the **Pittsburgh Reduction Company**, which took the name Aluminum Company of America in 1907. `fitted`

## The collapse

The price stories that survive checking are enough to show the shape, even where the individual points are thin:

| Year | Price (nominal US$) | Tag |
|------|---------------------|-----|
| 1852 | about $545 / lb | `assumed` |
| 1884 | about $1.10 / oz (about $16–18 / lb), roughly silver | `fitted` |
| 1888 | $4.86 / lb (Pittsburgh Reduction's launch) | `assumed` |
| 1893 | 78¢ / lb | `assumed` |
| 1897 | 36¢ / lb | `assumed` |
| 1900 | under 50¢ / lb | `assumed` |

From the Washington Monument year to 1900, the price fell **more than thirtyfold**. Measured from the 1850s it fell by three orders of magnitude, though the exact multiple depends on endpoints that still need a direct read of the United States Geological Survey's historical series. `assumed`

The metal moved into ordinary goods quickly. Pittsburgh Reduction was selling cooking utensils in the 1890s, and the Aluminum Cooking Utensil Company, the Wear-Ever brand, formed in 1901. In 1910 Robert Victor Neher patented continuous rolling of aluminum foil in Switzerland, and within a year it was wrapping chocolate. `fitted` The last public showpiece came at the edge of the collapse: in 1893 the figure atop the Shaftesbury Memorial Fountain at Piccadilly Circus, usually called Eros, was cast in aluminum, among the first statues ever cast in it. `fitted` Fifty years after aluminum was a Paris exhibition piece, it was cookware and candy wrappers.

## Solid electricity

The atoms did not change. The process did, and the process runs on electricity.

A modern smelter uses about **14 kilowatt-hours per kilogram** of aluminum. The International Aluminium Institute gives a global average of 14.2 kWh/kg for 2018. `fitted` That is why the industry follows cheap power, which in practice has usually meant hydropower: Iceland, Quebec, Norway, the Pacific Northwest. That clustering is standard industry geography but was not separately checked for this draft. `assumed` In energy terms, a bar of aluminum is a stored block of electricity.

The energy cost comes due only once. Recycling aluminum takes about **95% less energy** than making it from ore: the International Aluminium Institute's figures are 186 gigajoules per tonne for primary metal against 8.3 for recycled. `fitted` The cost is in getting the metal out of the oxygen. Once it is out it can be remelted, and the old cost stays paid.

The process also depended on cryolite. For most of the industry's first half-century, much of the world's natural cryolite came from one deposit at **Ivittuut, Greenland**. During the Second World War it was the only commercial natural source. Shipments peaked in 1942 at 86,000 tonnes, and protecting the mine was among the reasons for the American presence in Greenland. It was mined until **1987**. Cryolite has been synthetic for decades. The Geological Survey of Denmark and Greenland documents the mine's history. `fitted`

## Why it doesn't rust

Aluminum is so reactive that a fresh surface oxidizes almost instantly. The oxide it forms is a hard, transparent film only a few nanometres thick, about **2–5 nm** in dry air, and it seals the metal underneath. `fitted` The property that kept aluminum locked in rock for all of history is the same one that keeps a can from corroding.

## What the story gets wrong

The checking turned up more garbled lore than the history itself. These are worth naming because each one is in circulation:

- **"Davy isolated aluminum."** He named it. Ørsted and Wöhler isolated it. `fitted`
- **"Aluminium is the British correction of Davy."** A reviewer coined it. Davy's own last spelling was *aluminum*. `fitted`
- **"It was worth more than gold."** By weight, briefly, in the early 1850s. By 1884 it was about the price of silver. `assumed` / `fitted`
- **"Napoleon III's aluminum cutlery" and "bars beside the crown jewels."** Widely repeated and unsourced. `assumed`
- **"The Washington Monument tip cost $X."** Four different figures circulate. `fitted` that they conflict.
- **"Julia Hall co-invented the process."** Contested in the scholarship, not settled either way. `fitted`
- **"Never found native."** Almost never. Microscopic native aluminium is a recognized mineral. `fitted`
- **"Rome had aluminum."** This is the story of Tiberius and the unbreakable bowl. In Pliny's *Natural History* (book 36), a flexible *glass* is invented in Tiberius's reign and the craftsman's *workshop is destroyed* to protect the value of metals. Pliny himself says the story is repeated more than it is authenticated. The beheading comes from Petronius's *Satyricon*, in Trimalchio's dinner-party tale, and from Cassius Dio. In every ancient version the material is glass. The aluminum reading is modern speculation from a 1990 Time-Life book, and Gerhard Eggert took it apart in *Skeptical Inquirer* in 1995. `fitted`

The Tiberius story is a myth about aluminum. What persists is the shape of the parable: someone makes a precious material common, and the people invested in its scarcity react. Aluminum ended up as its real-world example, by process rather than by craftsman, eighteen centuries later.
