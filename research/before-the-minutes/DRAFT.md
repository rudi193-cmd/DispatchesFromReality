# Before It Was in the Minutes

**Sean Campbell** · What the Federal Reserve's own record says, 2023 to 2026, read line by line · November 2026

*A question reaches the podium before the minutes write it down, and the words change before the policy does. Both can be read off the record itself, with every line cited, if you read the record and not the coverage of it.*

---

## Why read the record

Most of what a person hears about the economy is a summary of a summary. A newscast says the Federal Reserve cut rates; a friend says the dollar always rises when rates do; a newsletter says a company bought back so many billions of its stock. Each of these can be checked, but almost nobody checks them, because the record is long, dry, and spread across a dozen publishers.

So I built the record into one pile and read it with a tool that does not summarise. The pile holds 944 sources fetched once on 5 November 2026, each pinned by its SHA-256: the Federal Open Market Committee's statements, minutes, projections and press conference transcripts for 2023 to 2026, FRED's economic series, the Treasury's yield curves, the European Central Bank's rates, the IMF's and World Bank's country data, the Fama-French factor library, and from SEC EDGAR the filing index of every S&P 500 company with the latest annual report of the 55 largest. The tool is willow, a kernel that answers only with passages from the files, each with its file and line, and says so when the files hold nothing.

What follows is what fell out. None of it is a forecast. All of it can be checked.

## The path, as the statements say it

The Committee's own statements give the rate decisions of 31 meetings from February 2023 to October 2026. Read in order, they make a shape the coverage tends to blur. In 2023 the Committee raised the target range four times and held four. In 2024 it held five times and lowered it three times, from September. In 2025 it held five times and lowered it three times, again from September. In 2026 it held at 3-1/2 to 3-3/4 percent from January through July, then on 16 September decided to raise the target range by 1/4 percentage point to 3-3/4 to 4 percent, and on 28 October held there. Two members voted against that hold, Lisa D. Cook and Beth M. Hammack, who preferred to raise again.

That September decision was a raise. It is the single fact in this piece most likely to be repeated wrongly, because the two previous Septembers were cuts, and memory fills in the pattern.

## A question reaches the podium first

On 7 November 2024, two days after the election, a reporter from Axios, Courtenay Brown, asked Chair Powell at his press conference whether the uncertainties he had mentioned included "proposals that the President-elect has put out on the campaign trail," and named one: tariffs. Powell's answer, in the Fed's own transcript, was that he was "not referring to, to the new Administration's policies at all, nor will I today."

That is the first time the word appears in any press conference in the pile, which begins in February 2023. At the December 2024 press conference it appears eight times. The first meeting whose minutes record it is January 2025. From then on, the minutes of every meeting through September 2026 discuss tariffs: 166 mentions in 14 sets of minutes. The press conference transcripts carry 231.

The order matters more than the counts. The press asked before the Committee wrote anything down, the chair declined to answer, and the written record followed the questions by two meetings. A reader of the minutes alone would date the concern to 2025. A reader of the transcripts dates it to the week after the election.

## The words change before the policy does

The Committee has a vocabulary of its own. Set its words against how often the rest of the English language uses them, and the ones it uses far more than the world does stand out. Then date each one's first appearance in the minutes, and the vocabulary becomes a timeline of what the Committee was worried about.

- **Artificial intelligence** first appears in the minutes of May 2024, as a possible source of higher productivity.
- **Stablecoins** first appear in July 2025, in the section on vulnerabilities in the financial system.
- **Reserve management purchases**, a new balance-sheet tool, first appear in October 2025 and are in the minutes of every meeting after.
- **Buildout** first appears in January 2026, in one sentence: a few participants commented that "the financing of the AI-related infrastructure buildout in opaque private markets warranted monitoring." It appears 25 times in four sets of minutes after that.

Words also leave. **Restrictiveness**, the Committee's word for how hard its policy was pressing on the economy, appears from July 2023 and is last used in December 2025. **Disinflation**, the word for inflation cooling, appears in 19 sets of minutes and is last used in April 2026, in a sentence saying it would likely be appropriate to lower the target range once there were clear indications that disinflation was firmly back on track. At the press conferences the same word was last said in March 2026. In September the Committee raised the target range instead.

None of this predicts. A word falling out of use is not a signal anyone could trade on. But it shows, in the Committee's own sentences, a concern fading months before a decision that went the other way.

## The form changes with the chair

The press conference of 29 April 2026 is Powell's last in the pile; in it he congratulates Kevin Warsh on advancing out of the Senate Banking Committee. From June 2026 the press conferences are Chairman Warsh's, and at the July one he says: "As before, the policy statement conveys just the facts."

The statements bear that out in their length. Counting only the policy text of each statement (the paragraphs from the release time to the implementation note), the rate statements of 2025 and early 2026 run 302 to 375 words (the August 2025 announcement of the Committee's updated longer-run strategy is not a rate statement and is left out). From June 2026 they run 141 to 183. The policy text was cut roughly in half at the change of chair.

## The dollar did not do what the rule says

A common rule of thumb says the dollar rises when American interest rates rise against other countries' and when stock prices fall, because money looks for safety and yield. In 2025 the record shows that rule failing, and the Fed's own staff saying so.

By FRED's daily rates, the euro bought 1.0261 dollars on 2 January 2025, 1.1770 on 30 June 2025, and 1.1163 on 30 October 2026. The yen moved from 157.65 to the dollar to 144.17 and back to 157.47 over the same dates. The Fed's broad dollar index fell from 129.46 to 119.41 in the first half of 2025 and stood at 121.67 at the end of October 2026.

The minutes of May 2025 record the manager of the Fed's market operations noting that the dollar had depreciated "even though U.S. interest rates had risen more than foreign interest rates and prices of risky assets had declined, which historically have been associated with dollar appreciation." Market contacts put it down to foreign investors hedging against further dollar weakness, not selling their American assets, and the manager noted no evidence that they had sold material amounts. In January 2026 the minutes record the dollar falling markedly after reports that the Fed's New York desk had made requests for indicative quotes, called rate checks, on the dollar-yen exchange rate. In March 2026 it rose, supported by worse risk sentiment and by the United States being a net energy exporter. In September 2026 it weakened again as interest rate differentials narrowed.

The rule of thumb is not wrong. It just has exceptions, and the record dates them.

## Recoveries are longer than the slogan

"Stocks always come back" is true of every crash in the record and misleading about most of them. Robert Shiller's monthly series for the S&P index, which runs from 1871, gives how long each major fall took to regain its old peak in price alone, and how long after adjusting for inflation.

| Fall | Drop, monthly average | Back to the old peak, price | After inflation |
|------|------|------|------|
| 1929 | 85 percent | 300 months, in 1954 | 350 months |
| 1973 | 43 percent | 90 months | 175 months |
| 2000 | 44 percent | 81 months | 171 months |
| 2008 | 51 percent | 65 months | 73 months |
| 2020 | 19 percent | 7 months | 7 months |
| 2022 | 20 percent | 24 months | not reached before the series' inflation figures end |

Two cautions travel with the table. These are monthly averages, which hide the worst days: the fall of March 2020 was about a third, day to day, not a fifth. And they are prices only; dividends reinvested would have shortened every recovery. Even so, after 1973 and after 2000, a person who measured in what money could buy waited about fourteen years.

## What companies say about their people's retirement

The latest annual reports of the 55 companies can be sorted by which retirement words each holds most: pension wording (defined benefit, pension plan, pension obligations), 401(k) wording (defined contribution, savings plan, matching contributions), or retiree health. Pension wording leads in 38 of them, among them the oil majors, the utilities, the industrial firms, the large banks and Coca-Cola. 401(k) wording leads in 14, among them Costco, Walmart, Home Depot, Microsoft, Nvidia, Meta, Tesla and McDonald's. Retiree health leads in one, Southern Company. Six files hold none of the phrases. Some companies appear twice, because a long report is read in parts. The split is not a finding about who offers what, only about what each report talks about most. Read that way, the older and more unionised firms still carry the language of pensions, and the newer firms carry the language of the 401(k).

## Where the reading fails

A tool that only quotes is honest about what it finds, but it can be wrong about what it reads, and some of what it got wrong is as useful as what it got right.

- **The 500th character.** The kernel reads only the first 500 characters of a line, and the Fed's minutes are long paragraphs. Read without breaking those lines at their sentences, the first mention of tariffs in the minutes came back as March 2025, ten weeks late, and nothing said a passage had been cut. With the lines broken, it is January 2025, and the count of mentions rises from 75 to 166.
- **Ranges blur.** The kernel reads "3-3/4 to 4 percent" as the numbers 3, 3, 4 and 4, so a sentence about a hold at 3-3/4 to 4 percent is held by January 2026's statement, which says 3-1/2 to 3-3/4. The right statement is among the passages that hold it, but a reader has to open the citation, not trust the count.
- **A change is not a range.** One checker read "raised its quarterly dividend from $0.26 to $0.27" as a single range, and so flagged a correct summary's "$0.27" as missing. It was fixed during this work.
- **Menus are not prose.** A readability score of the statements measured mostly the website's menus around them, which is why the length count above uses the policy text alone.
- **Apostrophes split.** The transcripts use curly apostrophes, so "wouldn't" was counted as a word "wouldn", and a list of the Fed's distinctive words was mostly the names of reporters who ask the questions.
- **Contradictions are not all contradictions.** A check for passages that disagree about the same date or amount paired the unemployment rate with the two percent inflation goal. Two numbers in sentences that sound alike are not a disagreement.

## What this is and is not

It is a reading of one public record, the Fed's own, with every claim tied to a file and line in a pile anyone can rebuild. It is not advice, not a forecast, and not a judgement on any decision the Committee made. The vocabulary dates are dates of first appearance in this pile, which begins in 2023; a word that appears earlier in records outside it is not contradicted here. The minutes are dated by the meeting they record, not by the day they were released, which is about three weeks later.

The point is smaller and more useful than a forecast. A person who has heard that the Fed cut rates in September 2026 can find, in one sentence of one statement, that it raised them. A person told that stocks always come back in a few years can find the fourteen-year waits. The record is there. It answers if it is asked.
